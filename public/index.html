Downloads

If You Have Not Downloaded
The card only holds about 28 days, so anything older than that can be overwritten and lost for good. If you are overdue, tell your transport manager or operator now and get the card downloaded at the next chance. Do not delete, alter or swap anything to cover the gap. If days are already lost, keep any printouts and make a written note of the missing days and why, and give it to your operator. Gaps in your records are what DVSA looks for at a check.
Your Obligation as a Driver
Carry your records for the current day and the previous 28 days (56 days if you have made an international journey to or from the EU), hand them over when an enforcement officer asks, let your operator download your card, and report a lost, damaged or faulty card straight away. Downloading the card on schedule is the operator duty, but you need to make the card available.
Why Download the Data
Your driver card holds about 28 days of data. After that the oldest data is overwritten and lost for good, so a missed download can leave a gap you cannot fill. The data is also your proof that you followed the hours rules. An enforcement officer can check the current day and the previous 28 days, and DVSA can fine driver hours offences found in that period.
Law
The operator must download driver cards at least every 28 days and the vehicle unit at least every 90 days, and keep the data for at least a year. As a driver you must carry your records for the current day and the previous 28 days, hand them over when asked, and let the operator download your card. If you employ yourself as an owner driver, the operator duties are yours.
Company Policy
Anything stricter than the law, such as downloading weekly, handing your card in on set days, or checking your own cards. It is not the law, but your employer can act on it under your contract, for example with a disciplinary.
Penalties in Court
Failing to use a tachograph, failing to install one, or failing to hand over records when an enforcement officer asks: a Level 5 fine, which is unlimited in England and Wales.
Failing to make or keep records: a Level 4 fine, up to £2,500.
Making a false entry or altering a record, or the tachograph seal, with intent to deceive: a Level 5 fine, and up to 2 years in prison if tried in the Crown Court.
Penalties at the Roadside
DVSA can issue fixed penalties of £50 to £300 each for hours and tachograph offences, up to 5 at one check, including offences from the last 28 days. Unpaid ones go up by 50% in England and Wales. DVSA can also prohibit you from driving, immobilise the vehicle, or refer you or your operator to the Traffic Commissioner, who looks at whether you are fit to hold your licence.
Points
Fixed penalties for hours and tachograph offences are non-endorsable, so they carry no points. Points apply to roadworthiness offences, such as 3 points for defective brakes on a goods vehicle.

Sign Shapes and Colours

Circles give orders. A red ring mostly means you must not do something (a prohibition, such as a speed limit or no entry). A solid blue circle mostly tells you what you must do (such as turn left or keep left).

Triangles warn you of a hazard ahead, such as a bend, a junction, animals or a slippery road.

Rectangles give information or directions. All information signs are rectangles.

Plates under a sign add detail, such as a distance, a time or a vehicle type.

Direction sign colours: blue is a motorway, green is a primary route, white with a black border is a local or non-primary route, and brown is a tourist attraction. An aircraft symbol shows the way to an airport.

Town and village name signs (the sign as you enter a place) are usually white with black lettering. They are not direction signs, they do not set a speed limit, and they do not mean a 30 limit on their own. Any speed limit is shown on its own red ring sign, often on the same post. Some have extra plates, such as "Please drive carefully" or a twin town. A brown sign is different: it points to a tourist attraction.

Roadworks signs warn of works and temporary changes, such as lane closures and temporary speed limits.

What is enforceable? Orders (red ring, blue circle, signals, most road markings and speed limits in a red ring) are the law and can be enforced. Warning triangles and information signs are not orders. An advisory speed, such as a white or plain-circle limit on a motorway gantry, is not a legal limit, but you should follow it and can still be charged for driving too fast for the conditions. In the Highway Code, MUST means a legal requirement and should means advice. Ignoring a "should" is not an offence by itself, but it can be used against you in court if something goes wrong. A speed limit in a red ring is enforceable even if it is lower than the road limit, such as 50 on a 70 road.

In Wales many signs are in Welsh and English. Some older designs are still on the road. Sign artwork is from The Highway Code traffic signs on gov.uk, Crown copyright, used under the Open Government Licence v3.0.

A quick reference for goods vehicle drivers in Great Britain, checked against the DVSA guidance on gov.uk (last updated 1 July 2026). The Today tab follows these rules. Always check gov.uk for changes.

Which Rules Apply
Assimilated (formerly EU) rules apply to most goods vehicles with a maximum permissible weight, including any trailer or semi-trailer, of over 3.5 tonnes, unless an exemption or UK derogation applies. They also cover UK-EU journeys, and AETR rules cover other international journeys. GB domestic rules mostly apply to vehicles that are exempt from the assimilated rules, on journeys within Great Britain. Which one applies depends on the vehicle's size, construction and use, not how it is registered. The rest of this app uses the assimilated rules.
Weeks and Time Frames
  • Fixed week: 00:00 Monday to 24:00 Sunday. The 56 hour driving limit, the two 10 hour days and the two weekly rest rule use it. A working week is different: it starts when a weekly rest ends.
  • Fortnight: any two weeks in a row, 90 hours of driving.
  • Break: after 4h 30m of driving, a 45 minute break, or 15 minutes then 30 minutes.
  • Daily rest: starts within 24 hours of the end of your last daily or weekly rest. Multi-manning: 9 hours within 30 hours.
  • Weekly rest: starts no later than the end of six 24 hour periods after your last weekly rest.
  • Making up a reduced weekly rest, or extra driving after an unforeseen event: by the end of the third week after.
  • Records at the roadside: today and the previous 28 days (56 days for a UK-EU journey). Lost or faulty card: drive up to 15 calendar days, report and apply within 7 calendar days.
  • Downloads: driver card at least every 28 days, vehicle unit at least every 90 days. Analogue charts back to the operator within 42 days.
  • Working time: 48 hours a week averaged over 17 weeks (26 with an agreement). Records kept for 2 years.
  • Roadside fixed penalty: pay within 28 days.
  • Digital tachograph clock: UTC. In British Summer Time it is an hour behind local time.
Driving Limits
Daily driving: 9 hours, up to 10 hours twice in a fixed week. Weekly: 56 hours. Any two weeks in a row: 90 hours. A fixed week runs from 00:00 Monday to 24:00 Sunday.
Breaks
After 4h 30m of driving take 45 minutes, or 15 minutes then 30 minutes. A break under 15 minutes does not count. Other work and POA are not breaks.
Daily Rest
Regular daily rest is 11 continuous hours within 24 hours of the end of your last rest. It can be split into 3 hours then 9 hours (12 in total). A reduced daily rest is at least 9 hours and under 11, allowed up to 3 times between weekly rests.

Short days: you need the same daily rest even if you only worked a few hours. Within every 24 hours from the end of your last rest you must have taken a new one. Worked 06:00 to 10:00? Your rest must start by 19:00 (or 21:00 for a reduced 9h) to finish by 06:00. A gap under 9 hours is a break, so working again later the same day is the same shift.

Rest and breaks: a daily or weekly rest also resets your 4h 30m of driving, because the 45 minute break is needed "unless he takes a rest period" (Article 7).
Weekly Rest
Regular weekly rest is at least 45 continuous hours. A reduced weekly rest is at least 24 hours and under 45, and must be made up by an equal rest taken in one block before the end of the third week after, attached to a rest of at least 9 hours. In any two fixed weeks you need two regular weekly rests, or one regular and one reduced. The next weekly rest must start within 6 days (144 hours) of the end of the last one. A regular weekly rest, or one with compensation attached, cannot be taken in the vehicle and must be in suitable accommodation paid for by the employer. A reduced weekly rest can be taken in a stationary vehicle with suitable sleeping facilities. Making up a reduced weekly rest: take the shortfall as one block of rest before the end of the third week after the week you took the short rest, attached to a rest of at least 9 hours. For example, a reduced rest in week 1 must be made up by the end of week 4.

Every job counts. A rest has to be clear of all work. Work in a job with no tachograph, work for another employer, and driving a vehicle outside the tacho rules (recorded as other work) all interrupt a rest.

International trips. On an international trip you may take two reduced weekly rests in a row if both are taken outside the UK and outside the country where you live. In any four fixed weeks you then need at least four weekly rests, at least two of them regular. The shortfall from both has to be made up before your next weekly rest and attached to it, and that next weekly rest must be a regular one. You must also get back to your base or home for a regular weekly rest once in every four weeks.

If it goes wrong. Graduated DVSA fixed penalties (up to £300 each), a Level 4 court fine, prohibition from driving until you rest, and possible referral to the Traffic Commissioner. DVSA said in 2017 it can fine up to £300 for a full weekly rest taken in the cab.
Ferry or Train
A regular daily rest on a ferry or train can be interrupted no more than twice, for no more than 1 hour in total. A weekly rest can be interrupted in the same way. To interrupt a regular weekly rest, the ferry or train leg must be at least 8 hours with a sleeper cabin. A reduced weekly rest needs a sleeper cabin, bunk or couchette to be available, and must still total at least 24 hours.
Two Drivers (Multi-Manning)
For the first hour of the period the other driver does not have to be with you. After that they must be. Each driver needs 9 hours of rest within 30 hours of their last daily or weekly rest. Together they can drive up to 20 hours before a daily rest, if both are entitled to 10-hour days.
Unforeseen Events
To reach a suitable stopping place, you may depart from the rules only as far as needed for safety, and road safety must not be put at risk. Write the reason on the printout or the back of the print roll, at the latest when you arrive at the stopping place. You still need your full daily rest afterwards, and any extension must be made up by the end of the third week. To reach your base or home to take a weekly rest, in exceptional circumstances: daily driving can go up to 10 or 11 hours, weekly driving to 57 hours and the 6 day limit by 1 hour. For a regular weekly rest only, daily driving can go up to 11 or 12 hours, weekly driving to 58 hours and the 6 day limit by 2 hours, with a 30 minute break before the extra time. The 90 hour fortnight limit still applies. Repeated use may suggest the work was not planned to fit the rules.
Working Time (WTR 2005)
Average 48 hours a week over 17 weeks (26 weeks with a collective or workforce agreement), up to 60 hours in any one week, with no opt-out. By default the 17 week periods are fixed, starting on the first Monday on or after 1 April, 1 August and 1 December. Annual leave and sick leave count as 8 hours a day. Ask your manager which period your company uses. No more than 6 hours of work without a break: 30 minutes for 6 to 9 hours, 45 minutes for more than 9 hours, in parts of at least 15 minutes. If you work at night (00:00 to 04:00) you must not work more than 10 hours in any 24. POA is known in advance and is not working time. A collective or workforce agreement can allow more than 10 hours at night. Employers keep records for 2 years. Self-employed drivers have been covered since May 2012. Drivers on GB domestic hours (and occasional mobile workers) fall under the 1998 Regulations and can opt out of the 48 hour average.
GB Domestic Rules
Up to 10 hours driving and 11 hours on duty in a working day, with no set break or rest times but your employer must make sure you get adequate rest. A working day is the 24 hours from when you start duty. You are exempt from the daily duty limit on days you do not drive, and for the whole week if you do not drive more than 4 hours on each day of that week. You need a written weekly record sheet if your vehicle needs an operator licence and you drive more than 4 hours in a day, or a tachograph instead. These rules only apply in Great Britain.
Tachograph Records
At the roadside you must produce records for today and the previous 28 days, or 56 days if you made an international journey to or from the EU. Digital drivers need their own card and enough printer paper. Driver cards must be downloaded every 28 days and the vehicle unit every 90 days. Analogue charts go back to the operator within 42 days.
Lost, Stolen or Faulty Card
You may drive without it for up to 15 calendar days (or longer if needed to get the vehicle back to its premises), using two printouts a day (start and end), with no change of vehicle. Report it to DVLA and apply for a new card within 7 calendar days. A forgotten card cannot be replaced by manual records.
Penalties
See the Tacho tab and the info buttons on the Today tab for fixed penalties and court fines. Fixed penalties are graduated by seriousness. If you have no UK address you may have to pay a deposit on the spot and not drive on until you do.

Protecting your driving licence, tacho card and Driver CPC card. Figures are from the Highway Code and gov.uk and were checked in October 2026. Not legal advice. Amounts and rules change, so check gov.uk.

Does This Apply to My Vehicle?
  • Up to 2 tonnes laden weight and car-derived vans: speed limits as for cars (60 single carriageway, 70 dual carriageway and motorway).
  • Goods vehicles up to 7.5 tonnes laden weight, which includes most delivery vans over 2 tonnes: 50 single carriageway, 60 dual carriageway, 70 motorway (60 on a motorway if articulated or towing a trailer).
  • Goods vehicles over 7.5 tonnes laden weight: 50, 60 and 60 in England and Wales. In Scotland it is 40, 50 and 60.
  • Right-hand lane ban on motorways with 3 or more lanes: goods vehicles over 7.5 tonnes, anything towing a trailer, and goods vehicles of 3.5 to 7.5 tonnes that must have a speed limiter. A normal van up to 3.5 tonnes with no trailer is not covered.
  • Parking on a verge, pavement or land between carriageways: the ban applies to over 7.5 tonnes including any trailer, unless police allow it or you are loading or unloading.
  • Tyres: tyres over 10 years old must not be used on the front axles of goods vehicles over 3.5 tonnes. Large vehicles need at least 1 mm of tread across three-quarters of the tyre width. Cars and light vans need 1.6 mm.
  • Higher fines: the Highway Code gives a higher maximum for a goods vehicle (for example £2,500 instead of £1,000 for a hand-held phone). In law a van built to carry goods is a goods vehicle, so this should include delivery vans. The Highway Code table does not define the term, so check if you are charged.
  • Tachograph and EU drivers' hours: the gov.uk guidance covers vehicles with a maximum permissible weight, including any trailer or semi-trailer, of over 3.5 tonnes, so a van up to 3.5 tonnes towing a trailer that takes the combined weight over that is included. A van that is exempt usually follows the simpler GB domestic hours rules. Driver CPC is for C1 and C licences, so a car-licence van driver normally does not need it, but I did not verify that on gov.uk. Which rules apply depends on the vehicle's size, construction and use, so confirm with your operator or DVSA.
Points and Fines That Hit HGV Drivers
  • Hand-held phone use while driving: 6 points, a £1,000 fine, or £2,500 in a goods vehicle, and you can be disqualified.
  • The ban covers all hand-held use, even while stationary in traffic, and even if you only pick the phone up to dial.
  • Failing to have proper control or a full view of the road: 3 points and up to £2,500 in a goods vehicle.
  • Speeding: 3 to 6 points (or 3 on a fixed penalty), a £1,000 fine, or £2,500 on a motorway.
  • Traffic light offence: 3 points.
  • Driving a class you are not entitled to drive: 3 to 6 points.
  • Using a vehicle in a dangerous condition: 3 points, and for an LGV the fine is unlimited.
  • Driving with no MOT certificate: £1,000.
  • Seat belt: £500.
  • Twelve points within three years normally means disqualification.
New Drivers: the 6 Point Rule
  • For two years from passing your first driving test, 6 or more penalty points (including any from before you passed) means your licence is revoked automatically.
  • You then go back to a provisional licence and must pass another driving test.
  • This is counted from your first test, so a car test years ago may be the one that matters, and an HGV test later does not necessarily restart it.
  • A driver who exchanged a foreign licence and later passed an HGV test is covered by the special rules.
  • If you are unsure of your date, ask DVLA.
Driver CPC Card
  • You must complete 35 hours of periodic training every 5 years to drive a lorry professionally.
  • Driving professionally without Driver CPC can mean a fine of up to £1,000.
  • If you drive in Europe, the 35 hours must be International courses only, because National courses give a National Driver CPC that does not allow professional driving in Europe.
  • Drivers who only work in the UK can use National courses, or a mix.
  • Use the free gov.uk service to check your hours so far.
  • If your card has expired you must complete training to get back to work, and what you need depends on when you first got your licence.
  • Do not leave it to the last year.
Digital Tachograph Card
  • Lost, stolen, damaged or not working: tell DVLA immediately (0300 790 6109, Monday to Friday, 8am to 7pm) and apply for a replacement within 7 days.
  • A lost or stolen replacement is £19 and can be done online.
  • A damaged or faulty one is by post only.
  • A first card is £32 and a renewal is £19.
  • Apply to renew at least 15 working days before the card expires. An expired card cannot record, and you cannot drive on it.
  • Change of name: change your driving licence first, then send form D777B to DVLA.
  • Change of address: update the address on your driving licence only. The card record updates by itself and you do not get a new card.
  • See also the Hours Rules tab for what to do while you wait.
Roadside Penalties (DVSA)
  • Fixed penalties are graduated.
  • Driving time over 4.5 hours: under 1 hour over £100, 1 to under 2 hours £200, 2 hours or more £300.
  • Overloading: under 10% £100, 10% to under 15% £200, 15% or more £300 (5% leeway unless the excess is 1 tonne or more).
  • Examiners generally allow about 15 minutes of leeway on driving time, but may act if it is used repeatedly, and can take in offences from the last 28 days.
  • Pay within 28 days, or ask for a court hearing in the same time.
  • In England and Wales an unpaid penalty goes up by 50%.
  • A deposit equal to the penalties is needed if you cannot give a satisfactory UK address.
  • Hotels, B&Bs, employment agencies and solicitors usually do not count, so know your home address.
  • Offences too serious go to court, with a deposit of £500, £1,000 or £1,500.
  • Up to 5 notices per examiner at one check.
  • Hours and overload penalties carry no points, but some roadworthiness ones do.
Traffic Commissioner and Your Vocational Licence
  • DVSA can refer you to the Traffic Commissioner instead of, or as well as, prosecuting.
  • This happens where an offence might cast doubt on your repute as a driver.
  • The Traffic Commissioner can take action against your vocational licence.
  • Falsifying a tachograph record or tampering with a seal with intent to deceive is a criminal offence with up to 2 years in prison.
  • Never swap cards, drive without a card, or alter records.
Licence Renewal and Medical
  • DVLA sends the renewal form and the D4 medical report about 56 days before your lorry or bus entitlement runs out.
  • Renewing is free, but the doctor sets their own fee for the medical.
  • A doctor completes the medical, and an optician can do the vision section if your doctor cannot.
  • If you do not get the forms, ask at a participating Post Office.
  • Do not let the entitlement lapse.
  • Check the gov.uk renewal page for the current rules on how often you must renew and whether you may keep driving while a renewal is processing.
Assumptions to Drop
  • The speed limit is not the same for every lorry.
  • Over 7.5 tonnes in England and Wales is 50 on single carriageways, 60 on dual carriageways and 60 on motorways.
  • In Scotland it is 40 on single carriageways.
  • Many large vehicles have a speed limiter at 56 mph, but the limit on the sign still applies.
  • A hand-held phone is not allowed at the lights or in a queue.
  • Hazard lights are not allowed while driving, except on a motorway or unrestricted dual carriageway to warn of a hazard or obstruction ahead, such as a queue, and only long enough for it to be seen.
  • Anything over 7.5 tonnes must not park on a verge or pavement without police permission, other than to load and unload, and then the vehicle must not be left unattended.
  • Vehicles over 7.5 tonnes (and those towing a trailer) must not use the right-hand lane of a motorway with 3 or more lanes.
  • Do not leave a heavy vehicle or trailer on the road at night without lights.
  • A tyre must have at least 1 mm of tread across three-quarters of its width, and tyres over 10 years old must not be used on the front axles of goods vehicles over 3.5 tonnes.
  • Allow a four-second gap in a tunnel.

General tips for a newly qualified driver. These are not official guidance, so check gov.uk for the detail.

Before Your First Day
Have your licence, Driver CPC card and digital tachograph card with you. Ask for a familiarisation drive and a handover on the vehicle's controls, tail lift or fridge unit. Check who to ring if you break down or are running late.
Every Day
Do the walkaround check before the first drive, using the Walkaround tab, and report defects before you move. Put your mode on the tachograph correctly: driving, other work, POA or rest. Plan your breaks around the 4h 30m limit rather than leaving it late.
Staying Legal
Keep a note of how many 10-hour days you have used this week. Make sure your card is downloaded on time and record anything unusual, such as a ferry or an unforeseen delay, on a printout or manual entry.
Asking for Help
If you are unsure about a rule, ask your transport manager before you drive. It is better to stop and ask than to break the rules.
